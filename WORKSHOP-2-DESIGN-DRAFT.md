# Workshop 2 design draft: Hacked: Who Speaks for You?

## Starting point

The Swedish exercise idea centers on an unauthorized viewer entering a fictional person's account and finding followed accounts, reading and engagement history, group memberships, and contacts. Participants infer things the person did not share publicly, then discuss what changes when an app, employer, friend, political party, or the wider public has that information. Alex, 19, is only a placeholder character.

The English workshop description also mentions an altered proposal under the account holder's name. That is a related but distinct incident. This draft puts the private-traces investigation at the center and holds proposal alteration as a possible short final twist, rather than requiring two full simulations.

## What participants should leave understanding

**Core insight:** Ordinary private activity, when combined, can reveal or suggest a person's interests, relationships, and civic views. An intruder may draw confident conclusions from it even when some are wrong. Losing control over who can see or use that information can affect whether a person feels free to explore ideas, join groups, and speak.

By the end, participants should be able to:

1. Distinguish what the account data actually shows from what they merely infer.
2. Explain at least one way unauthorized access to private activity could affect a person's democratic participation.
3. Suggest a response by the account holder, the platform operator, and the consultation organizer that helps restore control and trust.

The session should invite participants to reason about the risk, not claim that every exposed person will stop participating. The strongest learning point is that privacy protects room to think, associate, and speak; it is not only about hiding information.

## App concept: the role-reversal reveal

The app is a short fictional account investigation, opened from a large QR code and readable web address on Robin's presenter screen.

1. **Enter the scenario.** A pair or small group chooses a fictional profile. The app says an unauthorized person has gained access. It gives them the account view that person could see, without teaching an actual break-in method.
2. **Explore four areas.** Browse a small, purposeful set of followed accounts, articles or posts read, group memberships, and contacts. Each item should be short enough to inspect on a phone. Some are genuinely informative; others are ambiguous.
3. **Build a dossier.** The group selects two or three conclusions an outsider might draw. The app asks them to mark each as an observed fact or a guess. It does not grade political views or collect personal answers.
4. **Switch perspective: the aha moment.** The screen changes to the account holder's perspective: "This is the story someone built about you from private activity you never chose to publish." It shows what the outsider got right, what remains uncertain, and at least one plausible wrong inference. It then asks: "Would you still join this consultation or explore a new view if this could be seen by others?"
5. **Return to the room.** The app displays one closing question and stops. The facilitator leads the actor and responsibility discussion. A very brief altered-proposal card could be added later if Robin wants the title's impersonation dimension made explicit in the app.

The moment to protect is step 4. Do not explain the full reveal during the opening presentation.

## Smallest build and facilitation needs

- Static phone-friendly app with fictional content, no sign-in, no backend, no saved responses, and no real participant data.
- Presenter view with large QR code and readable link. A shared device per pair is enough; provide printed account cards if Wi-Fi or phones fail.
- Four short account sections, a fact-versus-guess selection, and one role-reversal reveal. The actor comparison can live in facilitator prompts rather than additional app screens.
- The facilitators need a script for the opening, reveal, debrief, and closing. Exact group count, age range, language, and final timetable remain unconfirmed.
