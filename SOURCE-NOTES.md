## Later workshop framing supplied by Robin

**Workshop 1, conference context:** Manipulated information can affect who participates in a democratic process and whose views influence the outcome. Formal access to a meeting is insufficient when people receive the wrong time, misleading information, or only one side of the story. Proposed activity: different youth groups prepare for a fictional municipal consultation with different information. Focus subjects are information manipulation, access and inclusion, source checking, and representation and responsibility. Central question: **Who influences a decision when some people have been misled or left out?** Robin's personal work remains focused on Workshop 2.

**Workshop 2, Robin's focus:** Unauthorized access to private information or changes to something submitted in a person's name can affect freedom to express opinions, join groups, and contribute to decisions. A newer proposed exercise has participants use a fictional youth consultation platform as Alex, 19, submit a proposal, inspect stored private activity, see a simulated incident that exposes information and alters the proposal, then decide how the person and platform should respond. Focus subjects are privacy and digital traces, account security, integrity and impersonation, freedom to participate, and shared responsibility among the individual, platform operator, and consultation organizer. Central question: **Can you participate freely if your private activity can be exposed or someone else can speak in your name?** Alex remains provisional given Robin's earlier hesitation.

## Workshop 1 concept: society and democratic influence

Central question: what happens to democracy when digital information and systems affect whose voices are heard?

Proposed simulation:

1. Introduce what meaningful democratic participation requires.
2. Divide participants into fictional youth associations, each with a short background.
3. Present a municipal decision, such as whether to build a youth center.
4. Give groups different information environments: varying news feeds, no invitation, wrong meeting time or location, or other barriers to attendance. The source also mentions travel difficulty as an example of a non-digital barrier.
5. Hold a meeting where each group states its position and reasoning.
6. Debrief: who could really influence the decision? Was the process democratic if everyone formally had a right to attend? What if a majority missed key information? What changes when some groups know more than others? Who is responsible for access to information? Is absence evidence of disinterest? How could the process be improved?

Optional experiment: groups inspect a simulated forum or feed whose content changes with their clicks, then compare what each group thought the general public believed. This is an idea, not a chosen technical implementation.

## Workshop 2 concept: individual participation and exposed information

Central question: what happens to a person's democratic freedom when information about them can be used against them?

Proposed controlled scenario: participants inspect a fictional account belonging to Alex, age 19, after being told that access has been compromised. The concept mentions followed accounts, articles and political posts read or engaged with, group memberships, and people Alex communicates with. Guiding questions could ask participants what they can infer. They then discuss why access to information Alex did not make public could matter.

Proposed discussion: how does the answer change when the viewer is an advertising app, employer, friend, political party, or the public after a leak? The scenario should use entirely fictional data and an intentionally contained exercise. No live account access is part of the brief.

## Wider topic bank from the supplied attachment

The supplied text begins with disinformation, false information, and the responsibility of digital platforms. It proposes three possible framings: digital democracy under pressure; whether people can trust what they see online; and cybersecurity as a foundation for democracy.

Its starting premise is that a functioning democracy depends on people's practical ability to access trustworthy information, express views without threats, communicate and organize, and protect their privacy and identity. It develops eight areas:

1. **Trustworthy information:** compromised news or organization accounts, fake sites, impersonation, deepfakes, coordinated disinformation, stolen or manipulated information, and harmful AI-generated content. The notes emphasize that confusion alone can erode trust.
2. **Safe expression:** harassment, threats, doxxing, account compromise, hate campaigns, impersonation, and fear that old posts will be used against someone can deter young people from speaking even when they formally have that right.
3. **Privacy and independent thought:** services may collect political interests, location, relationships, searches, vulnerabilities, and viewed or shared content. Privacy can give people space to explore ideas and organize without undue surveillance or manipulation.
4. **Social engineering:** phishing, fake invitations or sign-in pages, urgent messages, and impersonation of trusted people or institutions can target activists, youth organizations, journalists, campaigns, institutions, and community leaders.
5. **Protection of civic organizations:** attacks on youth groups, local associations, journalists, schools, nonprofits, researchers, or activists can expose member data, interrupt work, damage trust, block communication, or create fear.
6. **Trust in institutions and processes:** outages, compromised official accounts, leaked information, false official-looking messages, or false claims of a breach can undermine confidence even when direct technical damage is limited.
7. **Digital inclusion:** unequal access to devices, skills, language, fraud awareness, privacy-setting knowledge, or support can exclude people as participation moves online. This was marked as relevant to Workshop 1 and to practical teaching rather than expert-only instruction.
8. **Limits of security measures:** surveillance, censorship, unnecessary data collection, blocking legitimate expression, weakened encryption, or exclusion from digital services can themselves harm democratic participation.

These are possibilities to select from, not a checklist to fit into the two sessions.
